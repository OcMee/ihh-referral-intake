# Homelessness help in California and Orange County: the landscape

Researched 2026-10-17 with web search. **Important caveat:** the page-fetch tool could not reach
most sites from this environment (DNS errors), so most facts below come from search-result
summaries of the cited pages, not a full read of each page. Check a figure against its URL before
putting it on a slide. Anything not confirmed is marked "not found".

## Summary (for the pitch)

1. Orange County counted **6,321** people experiencing homelessness in January 2026 (3,256 sheltered, 3,065 unsheltered), down 13.7% from 7,322 in 2024; for the first time more people were sheltered than unsheltered.
2. California had about **181,934** people experiencing homelessness in HUD's 2025 count (about 115,583 unsheltered), roughly a quarter of the US total.
3. In OC the front door is **Coordinated Entry**: call **2-1-1** or go to any Access Point (shelter, outreach team, navigation center). People are assessed, then prioritized on a Community Queue by need, not by waiting time.
4. Medi-Cal (CalAIM) pays for **recuperative care** and other housing-related "Community Supports" through health plans; in OC that plan is **CalOptima Health**, which offers all 14 Community Supports.
5. **Big change coming:** DHCS will replace the recuperative care definition on **January 1, 2027** (room and board removed from the covered service, stronger supervision requirements) and will **sunset short-term post-hospitalization housing** as a separate service. Referral criteria and documents may change.
6. Hospitals must follow **SB 1152** (2019) when discharging homeless patients (written plan, meal, clothing, medication, screening, transport, a log of destinations). Reported pain points are slow, plan-by-plan approvals and not knowing where open beds are. A referral triage tool targets exactly that step.

## 2. How a person gets help in Orange County (plain words)

1. **First contact.** Someone calls **2-1-1** (211OC runs the "virtual front door"), or meets an outreach worker, a shelter, a navigation center, a food pantry, or a hospital social worker. Any of these can be a CES Access Point ("no wrong door").
2. **Assessment.** The Access Point assesses the household and helps collect documents (ID, proof of homelessness, income, disability if relevant).
3. **Queue.** The household is placed on a CES **Community Queue**. Priority is set by need; time on the queue does not raise priority (it is not a waitlist).
4. **Shelter in the meantime.** Staff can see and reserve open emergency shelter beds through the county's **Shelter Bed Reservation System** (individuals: Office of Care Coordination; families: Family Solutions Collaborative).
5. **Health and behavioral health.** Outreach teams (HCA Outreach & Engagement, street medicine, city outreach contractors) link people to clinics, mental health and substance use care. Medi-Cal members can get ECM care managers and Community Supports through CalOptima Health.
6. **Housing match.** When a housing resource opens (rapid rehousing, permanent supportive housing, vouchers), CES matches it to the highest-priority eligible household.

## 3. Medi-Cal / CalAIM

**Enhanced Care Management (ECM):** a statewide Medi-Cal benefit. A care manager coordinates
medical, behavioral and social care and meets members where they are (street, shelter, clinic,
home). "Individuals and families experiencing homelessness" is a population of focus.
Sources: https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/CalAIM/Documents/ECM-and-CS-Fact-Sheet.pdf ;
https://caloptima.org/en/About/CurrentInitiatives/CalAIM/ECM

**Community Supports:** 14 pre-approved services health plans may offer instead of costlier care.
The housing-related ones:

**Changes now under way (high relevance to IHH):**
- DHCS addendum to the Community Supports Policy Guide Vol. 2: **new recuperative care definition effective 2027-01-01**; current definition stays through 2026-12-31. Reason: the CalAIM 1115 waiver ends 2026-12-31, and recuperative care moves to "In Lieu of Services and Settings" (ILOS) authority. **Room and board is removed** from the covered service; services may become more clinically intensive; "enhanced supervision requirements"; DHCS says it will update the rate. **STPHH is sunset** as a separate Community Support. Feedback was due 2026-10-16.

## 6. Hospital discharge: law, gaps, pain points

**SB 1152 (2018, Health & Safety Code 1262.5, effective 2019-01-01).** General acute, acute
psychiatric and special hospitals must have a written homeless patient discharge policy. Before
discharge they must document that they:
- helped identify a post-discharge destination, with priority for a sheltered destination with supportive services;
- offered a meal, and weather-appropriate clothing if needed;
- gave a prescription and, if there is an onsite pharmacy, an appropriate supply of necessary medication;
- referred to follow-up care if medically necessary;
- offered or referred infectious disease screening and offered appropriate vaccinations;
- helped with insurance enrollment;
- offered transportation to the destination within 30 miles or 30 minutes.

Since 2019-07-01, hospitals must also keep a **log** of homeless patients discharged and their
destinations, and an annually updated written **coordination plan** with county behavioral health,
social services, health providers and nonprofits, including local shelter information.
Sources: CDPH AFL 19-01 https://calhospital.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/afl-19-01.pdf ;
https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CHCQ/LCP/Pages/LNCAFL19.aspx ;
https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/health-care-counsel-blog/california-increases-hospitals-obligations-when-discharging .
Open question: whether it covers ED visits or only inpatients (commentators disagree).

**Reported pain points:**
- After CalAIM started in 2022, San Diego's three main recuperative care providers reported a **50% drop in referrals**. Causes cited: days-long Medi-Cal authorization steps that differ by plan, and no clear view of which beds are open and staffed. https://voiceofsandiego.org/2024/03/07/despite-need-care-facilities-for-homeless-patients-are-underutilized/
- Capacity: one north San Diego County provider with 32 beds estimated a need for 50-100 more. Same source.
- LA hospitals reported keeping homeless patients up to four days longer than medically necessary for lack of post-acute options (2012 HASC report). https://archive.hasc.org/sites/main/files/file-attachments/healthcare_for_the_homeless_exec_summ.pdf
- People leaving recuperative care may be discharged without permanent housing (the problem the IHH/CalOptima/HCA pilot targets). https://www.chcs.org/resource/connecting-people-in-recuperative-care-to-health-and-housing-resources-lessons-from-orange-county-california/
- Orange County-specific data on referral turnaround or denial rates: not found.

## How a referral tool fits in

- **Faster, more consistent first read.** The documented bottleneck is the hand-off from hospital to recuperative care (approval steps, back-and-forth). A tool that checks a packet against IHH's admission rules and lists what is missing can shorten the loop without making the decision.
- **Chart over form.** SB 1152 forces hospitals to document many items; a tool can check whether the packet shows them (meal, medications, screening, destination) and flag "unknown" instead of assuming.
- **Ready for 2027.** The new recuperative care definition (enhanced supervision, no room and board, STPHH sunset) will change what counts as appropriate. Rules kept as data with IDs make it easy to update criteria when DHCS publishes the final text.
- **Audit trail.** DHCS asks plans to monitor that Community Supports providers actively assess appropriateness. A tool that records each rule, the evidence found and the staff decision supports that kind of review.
- **Next step after discharge.** A triage result could point staff to the CES path (2-1-1, Access Points) and ECM/housing navigation so recuperative care does not end without a housing plan. Staff decide; the tool only suggests.
